Lawn Care Pricing: Price by Time, Not Guesswork

Most solo operators price a lawn exactly once — a drive-by quote, a lot-size guess, maybe a number a competitor charges — and then never touch it again. Meanwhile the yard changes: grass grows in, beds get added, a fence goes up that adds five minutes of trimming every visit. The price doesn't move with it. That gap is where your margin quietly leaks out.

A "$/lawn" price hides the real number

A flat per-mow price feels simple, but it's hiding the number that actually matters: dollars per hour. A $45 mow that takes 20 minutes is $135/hour. A $45 mow that takes an hour because of a slope, tight gates, and overgrown edges is $45/hour. Both show up identically on your invoice list. Only tracked time tells them apart.

Reprice from data, not memory

You don't need a spreadsheet audit to fix this — you need the actual minutes spent at each property, collected automatically over a few weeks. Once you have that, repricing stops being a guess: sort every job by $/hour, find the ones dragging below your target rate, and you know exactly which quotes are stale.

What to do with an underpriced job

  • Raise the price if the job is otherwise fine — it just costs more time than it did when you quoted it
  • Fix the root cause if there's a specific reason it runs long (an overgrown bed, a gate you have to unlock and relock, an access path that eats five minutes)
  • Drop the client if neither of those closes the gap and it's just a bad account

Prices drift with the season, not just the client

Spring growth spurts, a dry August, a wet fall — the same yard takes different time in different months. A single annual price averages all of that away. Operators who track time per visit notice the drift early, instead of discovering at tax time that half their route was quietly unprofitable for two months.
