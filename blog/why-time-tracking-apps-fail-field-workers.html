Why Most Time-Tracking Apps Fail Field Workers

Most time-tracking software is built by people who sit at a desk, for people they assume also sit at a desk. That assumption breaks the moment you try to use one outside — and if you run a mowing crew, a landscaping outfit, or any trade that lives in a truck, you've probably felt it.

Gloves don't work on a touchscreen built for fingertips

Small tap targets, tiny text fields, dropdown menus that need precision — all of it assumes bare hands and a stable desk. In the field you're wearing gloves, standing on uneven ground, and you need to clock in with one thumb while carrying a trimmer in the other hand. A tool built for the field has to have big, forgiving buttons and almost nothing to type.

Screens weren't designed for direct sun

Dark UI themes, low-contrast gray-on-white text, thin fonts — they all vanish at noon in July. Field-first design means high contrast by default, because "readable in a conference room" and "readable standing in a driveway at 1pm" are two different problems.

No signal doesn't mean no work

Rural properties and job sites in dead zones don't pause because your app needs a connection. If a time tracker can't log an arrival offline and quietly sync later, you lose the entry — and the trust. Offline-first isn't a nice-to-have for a mobile trade; it's the baseline.

Zero desktop bloat, by design

A one-person or small crew doesn't need a login portal, a settings wizard, or a dashboard meant for an office manager. It needs:

  • One tap to start — no menus between arriving and clocking in
  • GPS that confirms, not interrogates — you shouldn't have to prove where you are
  • Reports that export cleanly — without a spreadsheet cleanup pass afterward

That's the whole design brief behind MowClock: built outside, for people who work outside.
